Problem Definition — How Might We?

How Might We? Agent

We now need to turn Fiona's pain into a question we can answer. The How Might We? (HMW) method is a simple way to do that — you phrase the problem as an open question starting with "How might we…". For example: "How might we help Fiona spend less time on false positives?"

01

What is it?

A simple phrasing trick: start every problem statement with "How might we…". It turns a complaint into something you can actively work on.

02

Why does it work?

How assumes a solution exists. Might gives permission to be wrong. We makes it a team effort. Together, they unlock ideas.

03

What will you have at the end?

One clear HMW question that captures the real problem — the thing you'll actually build an agent to solve.

04

How do you do it?

Three steps: generate lots of HMW questions, group the similar ones, pick the best one. Then hand off to ideation.

Step 1

Get grounded in Fiona's world

Before writing any HMW questions, make sure everyone in the room shares the same picture of the problem. Recap who Fiona is, what her day looks like, and which pains matter most. Get a mix of perspectives around the table — the more varied the group, the broader the questions you'll generate.

Step 2

Generate lots of HMW questions

Individually, write as many "How might we…" questions about Fiona's problem as you can. Aim for quantity, not perfection — weird ones are welcome. Then share with the group and build on each other's ideas.

Golden rule: the question should not contain a solution. Good: "How might we help Fiona focus on the alerts that matter?" Bad: "How might we build an AI filter for Fiona's alerts?" — the second one has already picked the answer.

Template: How might we [verb] [topic] for [user]? — e.g. "How might we reduce escalation time for Fiona?"

Step 3

Group the similar ones & pick a winner

You'll end up with 15–30 HMW questions. Group the ones that are really asking the same thing — this is called clustering. Then as a group, vote on the question that best captures the real problem.

Worked example. Imagine you wrote these five HMWs about Fiona:

  • How might we help Fiona finish her queue faster?
  • How might we reduce the number of false positives Fiona sees?
  • How might we help Fiona write escalation notes more quickly?
  • How might we help Fiona spot the real alert hidden in the noise?
  • How might we help Fiona draft her MAR report faster?

Questions 1, 2, and 4 are all really about triage speed — they cluster together. Questions 3 and 5 are both about drafting written outputs — that's a second cluster. You now have two clear problem areas to choose from, not five scattered ones.

Step 4

Hand off to the AI Matrix

Take your winning HMW question into the AI Matrix next — that's where we'll generate candidate agents to answer it, and pick the one we build live.

Ideation — AI Matrix

AI Matrix Agent

The AI Idea Matrix is a version of the Creative Matrix with the columns pre-filled with AI capabilities, reframed as human senses and actions: See, Read, Listen / Hear, Connect, Predict / Rationalise, and Touch / Robotics. Thinking about AI this way makes it easier to imagine concrete applications — and to spot opportunities you'd miss if you were just listing models.

Step 1

Break down the HMW into pain points

A pain point is a specific moment of friction in Fiona's day — something small, concrete, and observable. Good pain points are things you could watch over her shoulder and see. Bad pain points are vague statements like "she's overwhelmed" or "the system is old".

To find them, take your winning HMW question and ask: "What are all the moments that make this hard for Fiona?" Individually, list everything you can think of — aim for 10–20.

Worked example. HMW: "How might we help Fiona focus on the alerts that matter?"

Pain points a new analyst might list:

  • The alert queue doesn't show which alerts are most urgent.
  • She has to re-read the same typology rules every time.
  • She can't tell at a glance if a client has a repeating pattern.
  • Alerts don't carry context from the previous day.
  • She types the same escalation rationale over and over.
  • The MAR deadline clock is always in her head.
  • The L3 reviewer pushes back on weak rationale.
  • Half her morning is spent on alerts she's already seen cleared before.

Notice: each one is a specific task, moment, or blocker — not a vague feeling.

Once everyone's list is shared, cluster the pain points into 3–5 problem areas. These become the rows of your matrix.

How do I cluster, in practice?

The idea: some of your pain points are secretly talking about the same underlying problem. Clustering is just putting those together and giving each group a name.

How to do it:

Step 1

Put every pain point on its own sticky

Physical stickies on a wall or digital cards on a Mural/Whiteboard — either works. One pain point per sticky. Don't try to sort them yet.

Step 2

Read one sticky, then look for its siblings

Pick any sticky. Ask: "Which other stickies are really about the same thing?" Move them next to each other. Don't worry about naming the group yet — just let the shape emerge.

Step 3

Keep going until nothing's left alone

Some stickies will cluster fast. Others won't fit anywhere — that's a signal they might be their own cluster, or they might not be pain points at all. Discuss as a group.

Step 4

Name each cluster in plain English

Give each group a short, neutral label — not a solution. "Queue prioritisation" is a good cluster name. "AI ranking system" is not, because it's a solution already.

Worked example — Fiona's pain points, clustered

Taking the 8 pain points from Step 1 above:

Cluster A · Queue prioritisation

• Alert queue doesn't show urgency  • Can't tell if a client has a repeating pattern  • Half her morning on already-cleared patterns

Cluster B · Context & memory

• Re-reads typology rules every time  • Alerts don't carry context from the previous day

Cluster C · Drafting & defending decisions

• Types the same rationale over and over  • MAR deadline pressure  • L3 reviewer pushes back on weak rationale

Eight scattered pain points just became three clear problem areas — the rows of Fiona's AI Matrix.

Step 2

Read across the AI senses

For each problem area, work down the columns and ask what each AI capability could unlock:

  • See — image and video understanding (e.g. chart reading, document scanning).
  • Read — text comprehension, extraction, classification.
  • Listen / Hear — speech, call transcription, audio signals.
  • Connect — linking, retrieval, knowledge graphs, search.
  • Predict / Rationalise — forecasting, reasoning, decision support.
  • Touch / Robotics — physical action and process automation.
Non-AI ideas are welcome — capture them separately. Not every problem needs a model.
Step 3

Combine & prioritise

Look across the matrix for ideas that can combine across capabilities. Vote to surface the strongest candidates.

Step 4

Pressure-test the winner

For your top idea, answer two questions before you build:

  • Can we combine AI technologies here into a single cohesive solution?
  • What data do we need to make it work?
